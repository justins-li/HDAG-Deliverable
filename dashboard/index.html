Route detail
Dials are percentile ranks within the 307-route pool, so they are unit-free and additive. Source: USDOT T-100 International Report, Oct–Dec only. This is the one month window present in both the passenger and departure files in every year, 1990–2019. Totals are Q4 volumes, never annual ones.
What the columns mean
The framing

Meridian gets one rotation, so maximising market size is the wrong objective. The biggest markets are big because incumbents with slots, feed and scale already hold them. Opportunity here is demand that already exists, on planes that are already full, on a route nobody owns. The four dials score exactly that.

D · Demand Q4 passengers, 2015–19

Is the market real? Total Oct–Dec passengers over the five most recent years. A floor, not a maximand. The top decile by volume was already cut, because the client refuses the biggest markets.

G · Growth vs 2010–14

Is it getting bigger? Change in Q4 passengers against the prior five-year block. One rotation is a long bet, so direction matters. Treat very large percentages with suspicion. They come off small bases.

S · Strain passengers ÷ departures

Are the planes full? The data reports passengers and flights but never seats, so this is a load proxy, not a load factor. High strain means existing service runs full, so added capacity gets absorbed instead of starting a price war.

R · Room 1 − HHI of carrier passenger shares

Can we get in? HHI is the sum of squared carrier shares: 1.00 is a monopoly, lower is more fragmented. We use concentration rather than carrier count deliberately. Two carriers can be an open duopoly or a 97% fortress, and only HHI tells them apart. It is what keeps hub fortresses like ATL–AMS out of the shortlist.

TAI · Thin Air Index weighted mean of D, G, S, R

Each dial is a percentile rank inside the candidate pool, then weighted by the sliders. The bar shows each route's score against the current leader. Percentile rather than z-score because the underlying distributions are badly right-skewed.

Carrier mix donut, right-hand panel

Passenger share by carrier, 2015–19. This is what HHI measures, drawn. One fat wedge is a fortress. Carriers past the top three are pooled as “Other”.

Q4 passengers by year line, right-hand panel

Oct–Dec passengers, 2010–19. On JFK–MAN this is where the case lives: the fall into 2019 is Thomas Cook's September 2019 collapse taking 47% of the route's seats with it.

What this screen cannot see

Slots, bilateral traffic rights, gate access and immunised joint ventures. That blind spot is why the top-scoring route (LAX–HND, into slot-controlled Tokyo Haneda) is rejected by hand rather than by the index.
