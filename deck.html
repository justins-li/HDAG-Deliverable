Meridian gets one rotation. That rules out maximising market size. The biggest markets are big because incumbents with slots, feed and scale already hold them. So we scored routes on capturability, not size:

Opportunity is demand that already exists, on planes that are already full, on a route nobody owns.

The funnel

4,398 airport pairs → 5 finalists

  • Material & scheduled: ≥50k Q4 passengers, present all five years, ≥80% scheduled. A rotation needs a repeating market, not charters.
  • Niche: drop the top decile by volume. This is the client's instruction, applied as a rule rather than argued from evidence.
  • Growing: demand rising against 2010–14.
  • Contestable: ≥2 carriers and HHI ≤ 0.90, which removes hub fortresses like ATL–AMS and DTW–AMS that look thin but are locked.
The index shortlists. It does not decide.

TAI ranks LAX–HND first. We reject it: Tokyo Haneda's US slots are allocated government-to-government and held by Delta, United, American and ANA. "Room" measures concentration. It cannot see that the seats are legally unobtainable. Three majors splitting a route evenly reads as contestable and is in fact a closed shop.

The recommendation

JFK ⇄ Manchester, and the reason is dated

Manchester is the UK's second gateway: the same national market as Heathrow, without the slot war Meridian cannot win. It is also the only finalist with a catalyst.

Thomas Cook collapsed in September 2019.

It carried 47% of the route in 2018. Virgin Atlantic backfilled only part of it. The planes still flew 320 passengers per departure, so the demand did not evaporate. It went unserved.

Position & honesty

SWOT, and what this analysis cannot see

Strengths

  • Two profitable years, capital in hand
  • No legacy network to defend; free to pick optimally
  • One rotation = small, reversible commitment

Weaknesses

  • No connecting feed, pure point-to-point
  • No slot portfolio at constrained airports
  • No alliance, JV or corporate contracts
  • Unknown brand in every foreign market

Opportunities

  • Carrier failures vacate proven demand
  • Secondary gateways are unslotted, under-served
  • Monopoly routes carry fat, attackable fares

Threats

  • Virgin adds frequency and squeezes us
  • The gap may already be backfilled
  • Leisure skew → thin premium cabin, weak yield
  • One route = zero diversification
Limitations of the data & method
  • The files are Q4-only, and the window moves. Passengers cover Sep–Dec then Aug–Dec; departures Oct–Dec then Sep–Dec. We forced a constant Oct–Dec window to kill the step artifacts, at the cost of the entire summer peak. Manchester is leisure-leaning, so its true annual demand is likely understated here.
  • The index cannot see non-concentration barriers: slots, bilateral rights, gates, immunised JVs. This is why the top-scoring route is rejected by hand.
  • Five-year HHI hides the present. JFK–MAN scores Room 0.70 across 2015–19, but in 2019 Virgin is the only carrier. We are entering a monopoly today.
  • Airport pair ≠ O&D market, so the addressable market is smaller than the counts imply.
  • Corporate parents are invisible. DY and DI on LAX–LGW are both Norwegian, scored as two competitors.

Assumptions: Q4 ordering approximates annual ordering; passengers ÷ departures is a usable load proxy absent seat counts; vacated demand persists rather than permanently re-routing via LHR/DUB/AMS; and history guides the launch year.
