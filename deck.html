Meridian Air · Route Network Strategy

Fly where the air is thin.

The board funded one rotation and refused the biggest markets. Ranking by volume returns JFK to Heathrow, which is slot-controlled and run as a four-carrier joint venture. Size is evidence that demand is already served. So we score capturability.

The definition

Opportunity is demand that already exists, on planes that are already full, on a route nobody owns.

Why each dial is a percentile, not a raw number

Passengers, a growth rate, people per flight and a concentration score cannot be added together. Ranking each route 0 to 100 within the candidate pool makes four different units additive. We rank rather than standardise because airline traffic is heavily right-skewed, and a handful of enormous routes would otherwise dominate any average.

Room uses concentration, not a count of airlines

Two carriers can be an open duopoly or a 97% fortress. Counting cannot tell them apart; HHI can. That single choice is what keeps Delta's ATL–AMS and DTW–AMS hub fortresses off the shortlist despite their thin carrier counts.

How the four combine

The Thin Air Index is the weighted mean of the four percentile ranks. Equal weights are a starting prior, not a claim: we had no basis for asserting that growth matters 1.4 times as much as strain, so we started neutral and then tested every alternative. Slide 5 shows what moving them does to the answer.

Where Meridian stands

One aircraft. No slots. No feed.

This is not background. The weaknesses on the right are the reason Strain and Room carry as much weight as raw demand: an airline with no slot portfolio and no connecting passengers has to win on capturability or not at all.

Strengths

  • Two profitable years, capital in hand
  • No legacy network to defend, free to pick optimally
  • One rotation is a small, reversible commitment
  • No hub to protect, so no cannibalisation

Weaknesses

  • No connecting feed. The route must work on its own traffic
  • No slot portfolio at constrained airports
  • No alliance, no joint venture, no corporate contracts
  • Unknown brand in every foreign market

Opportunities

  • Failed carriers vacate proven demand, not speculative demand
  • Secondary gateways are unslotted and under-served
  • Monopoly routes price high, which leaves margin to attack
  • Point-to-point leisure needs no hub feed

Threats

  • Incumbent adds frequency and squeezes the entrant
  • A vacated gap may already have been backfilled
  • Leisure markets skew thin on premium cabins and yield
  • One route means zero diversification
What this rules out

Slot-controlled airports such as Heathrow and Tokyo Haneda. Hub fortresses where one carrier holds the traffic. Anything that only works with connecting passengers.

What this points toward

Second-city gateways, routes a carrier has just exited, and markets where the planes still fly full despite thin service.

The four cuts
  • Material and scheduled. At least 50,000 Q4 passengers, present in all five years, at least 80% scheduled. One rotation needs a repeating market, not charters.
  • Niche. Drop the largest 10% by volume, above 595,034 Q4 passengers. This is the client's instruction applied as a rule, not a finding.
  • Growing. Demand rising against 2010–14.
  • Contestable. At least two carriers and HHI at or below 0.90. This is the cut that removes the hub fortresses.
The index shortlists. It does not decide.

The Thin Air Index ranks LAX–HND first. We reject it. Tokyo Haneda's US slots are allocated government to government under the bilateral treaty and held by Delta, United, American and ANA. Room measures concentration only, so three majors splitting a route evenly reads as healthy competition when it is a closed shop. The seats are not expensive, they are legally unobtainable.

We overrode the model using knowledge the data does not contain, and labelled the override rather than retuning weights until our preferred answer won.

Only one finalist has a catalyst. The other four are healthy but static: good demand, full planes, and no particular reason that now is the moment to enter.

The funnel is reversible. Every cut and threshold is logged in the notebook and reproducible from a clean kernel. The 302 contestable routes that did not reach the shortlist stay scored and rankable in the live screen, so any stage can be reopened and the thresholds moved.

The recommendation

JFK ⇄ Manchester. The reason is dated.

Manchester is the United Kingdom's second gateway: the same national market as Heathrow, without the slot fight Meridian cannot win. It is also the only finalist with a catalyst.

Thomas Cook collapsed in September 2019

It carried 38,749 Q4 passengers in 2018, or 47% of the entire route. Virgin Atlantic backfilled part of it and no more. Read the third column below: even after losing nearly half the seats, the remaining flights still went out with 320 people aboard. The demand did not evaporate. It went unserved.

Assumptions
  • Q4 ordering approximates annual ordering. We never claim Q4 volume is annual volume.
  • Passengers ÷ departures is a usable load proxy. The data has no seat counts.
  • Vacated demand persists rather than permanently rerouting via LHR, DUB or AMS.
Limitations
  • No summer in the data. Manchester leans leisure, so its true demand is likely understated here.
  • No revenue anywhere. We count people, never money. Full planes at low fares do not pay for a widebody.
  • Five-year HHI hides the present. JFK–MAN scores 0.28 across 2015–19, but in 2019 Virgin is the only carrier. We would enter a monopoly.
  • Airport pair is not a true origin and destination market.
Risks, and what would change our mind
  • Virgin responds. It has the aircraft and the incentive to refill the gap.
  • Yield, not volume. A leisure gateway may not support the fares.
  • The single finding that kills this: post-2019 schedule data showing Manchester capacity restored to 2018 levels. Our data stops in December 2019, three months after the gap opened. We can watch it open. We cannot watch it close.
